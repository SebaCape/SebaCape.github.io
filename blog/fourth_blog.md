## Puzzles

If you want to become a professional software engineer, it is practically an inevitability that at some point you will have solve some puzzles. Whether its for interview prep, or designing actual systems, the simple truth is that the problem solving muscle obtained from these types of challenges is necessary to be an effective engineer.

Within the software industry, the most common way that this is tested of people is through algorithmic programming (more commonly known as "Leetcode style," or DSA problems.) I personally have a fair amount of experience with these types of problems, having solved 600+ Leetcode problems in order to train myself for interviews. Additionally, I have recently begun trying to go further, dipping my toes into competitive programming via sites like Codeforces and USACO.guide. ## Building a Line of Reasoning

When you first encounter an algorithmic programming problem that fall within the class of those described above, you need to actually internalize what is being asked of you. After you know what is being asked, you can start thinking of approaches and then how to specifically apply them.

Codepath has a framework for this called UMPIRE (Understand, Match, Plan, Implement, Review, Evaluate,) but any shorthand for how to approach a problem will ultimately come down to the same case of figuring out what is being asked of you, and then deriving how to achieve that ask.

For algorithmic problems specifically, it is quite often that you will be given specific constraints as to what your inputs are limited to. These constraints more often than not throw you a bone regarding how they should be solved (e.g. input size -> time complexity -> algorithm or data structure to use.) Over time the recognition of these patterns becomes simpler, but there is a time where you are still learning them. There is a way to optimally approach problems that you can't *reasonably* solve with your knowledge base to learn these things as fast as possible (beyond just cramming random examples from a textbook.)

## Getting Mileage Out of the Unsolvable

As a general rule of thumb, I like to give myself 15 minutes to make any progress on an algorithmic problem. If I can't make *ANY* progress whatsoever in the problem (with progress being measured in any new code that touches the program, or any new idea that crosses the mind,) then I begin to do something I call breadcrumbing. Before I explain what this means, note that I am not saying to give up on anything you can't solve within 15 minutes; rather that it is often worthwhile (at least if you are only going as far as the proficiency required to pass interviews,) to give yourself some hints if you go for 15 consecutive minutes being stuck at any point.

Anyways, "breadcrumbing," what is it in this context? I'm not referring to the manipulative practice of only giving someone enough attention to keep coming back to you, but not enough to maintain a healthy relationship (yikes, that's a pretty unfortunate association for the word to have :\ ) What I am referring to is the act of revealing unforeseen information regarding a problem you are stuck on in order to assist yourself with making progress on it, or providing sequential "hints" to yourself.

## What Breadcrumbing Solves

Now, I think a large issue that people run into, is the fact that once you look at the solution to a problem, its value is expended. Once you read and understand that solution, once you grapple with it long enough, you can't quite go back to the state in which you previously were where you had to derive a lot of intuition on your own. As a result, the weight is lifted, and you can't use it to build as much muscle anymore. Because of this, looking at a solution all at once is an antipattern. It feels like good practice, but it is bad because you rob yourself of the critical thinking process necessary to get good at these types of things.

However, with "breadcrumbing," you only want to look to the very next thing that you are missing. Complexity only arises in not knowing what to do next, so if you could just give yourself the minimum viable unit of information necessary for the gears to start turning and new ideas to start developing, then this is going to be what helps you the most. You only want to look at part of a solution, or part of an editorial, and then take more time to think and try to implement on your own before you just steal the entire thought process from anybody else.

It is like going to the gym. When you are about to fail a rep, having a spotter that just lifts the weight the rest of the way for you doesn't do much for your personal growth. However, a good spotter will give you just enough support to ensure that you are safe, but still getting value out of your rep. Maybe you need guidance along the entire motion, maybe there is simply a break point that you need assistance in getting past. Regardless, the goal of an optimal spotter is to give you the smallest amount of assistance that they can, while still providing enough that you can continue to move forwards.

I see existing solutions and editorials to already solved problems that one tackles in a similar light. You don't want everything done for you at once; it takes away from the value of going through the motions and putting in the work to reach an endpoint. By practicing breadcrumbing as I have defined it, I think a lot of people can get a substantial amount more benefit out of their "unsolvable" problems. It just takes effort and a little bit of restraint and patience, but I can vouch that it is very much worth it in the end.
